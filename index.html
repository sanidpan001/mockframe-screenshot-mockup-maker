Why plain screenshots underperform

A screenshot is evidence; a mockup is communication. Drop a raw capture into a post, a landing page or a pitch deck and the viewer's eye slides off it — the edges are abrupt, the scale is wrong, and nothing signals what deserves attention. Wrap the same image in a recognisable device frame, give it breathing room on a considered background, and add the soft shadow that tells the brain 'this is an object in a scene', and engagement visibly changes. Product teams at major app companies employ people whose entire job is this transformation. MockFrame gives you the same transformation in about ten seconds, free, in your browser.

The device frame does specific work: it tells the viewer how the product is held and used. A phone frame says 'this lives in your pocket, glanceable and personal'. A laptop frame says 'this is where work happens'. A browser window says 'no install, it just runs'. Choosing the frame is therefore choosing the story — which is why MockFrame offers all three plus a frameless card for a clean, editorial look.

The anatomy of a good-looking mockup

Three ingredients separate polished mockups from garish ones: padding, restraint and shadow. Padding — the margin between the device and the edge of the image — is what makes a design feel expensive; cramped compositions feel like accidental screenshots. MockFrame's padding slider runs to 160 pixels so social exports can breathe. Restraint applies to backgrounds: a smooth two- or three-stop gradient in a family of related colours flatters almost any interface, while busy patterns fight the product for attention. The six built-in gradients are deliberately harmonious, from a deep ocean for developer tools to a warm sunset for consumer apps.

Shadow is the third ingredient and the easiest to overdo. A real object under soft studio light casts a diffuse shadow slightly below itself — exactly what the shadow-strength slider simulates with blur and offset. Keep it subtle: a shadow you notice is usually a shadow that is too strong. Corner radius matters too, because modern phone and browser chrome is rounded; matching your export's radius to the frame keeps the illusion intact. When all four settings agree, the result reads as a photograph of a product rather than a picture of a file.

Export sizes that match where images actually go

Every platform crops differently, and a mockup composed for one will be butchered by another. The size presets cover the workhorses: 1200×630 for link shares and blog headers, 1080×1080 for square feed posts, and 1080×1350 for portrait posts that occupy more vertical feed space — currently the most valuable rectangle on social media. Choosing a preset re-composes the canvas at that exact aspect, centring your device with its padding intact instead of stretching or cropping it after the fact.

A practical workflow: build the mockup once with your frame and gradient, then export at each preset you need. Consistency across sizes — same gradient, same device, same shadow — is what makes a set of images look like a brand rather than a folder of accidents. Save your favourite combination of settings; recreating it takes seconds and keeps every post in a campaign visually related.

Small craft tips that lift the result

Match the gradient to your product's own palette for instant brand coherence; the ocean and grape families flatter most blue-and-violet interfaces, while mint and rose suit health and lifestyle products. Use the phone frame at portrait presets and the laptop or browser frame at landscape ones — forcing a wide laptop into a square canvas shrinks the interface to illegibility. If your screenshot is itself dark, the charcoal gradient creates a premium, gallery feel; if it is light and airy, stronger colour behind it stops the composition washing out.

Finally, keep the screenshot itself honest. Mockups persuade best when they show the real product doing a real task with realistic data — invented dashboards and lorem-ipsum interfaces are spotted instantly by the audiences you most want to convince. Frame reality beautifully; do not replace it.

Framing screenshots for different jobs

A founder announcing a feature needs the phone frame, a bold gradient and the portrait export — the image must survive a fast-scrolling feed. A developer documenting an API needs the browser frame at landscape share size, where the window chrome quietly certifies 'this runs in a tab, nothing to install'. A teacher assembling slides needs the frameless card at high padding, because projection washes colour out and space reads as calm from the back row. Choosing the frame is choosing the audience's first inference, and it takes less time than reading this paragraph.

Build a tiny house style once you find settings you love — same gradient family, same padding, same frame per channel — and your screenshots become recognisably yours before a single word is read. That recognisability, compounded across a hundred posts, is what people mean by 'their content looks professional'. It was never a designer. It was a system, and yours is now set.

Batch consistency for teams

When a whole team ships screenshots — support articles, release notes, a sales deck assembled by five people — inconsistency is the tax everyone pays in mismatched gradients and random padding. Cure it socially, not technically: agree the house settings once (frame per surface, one gradient family, one padding value, one export size per channel), write them at the top of the team's style note, and every future mockup is a thirty-second errand instead of a design decision. MockFrame's controls map one-to-one onto such a note, which is deliberate: a tool that a team can standardise on beats a more powerful tool that every member configures differently. The brand coherence your audience perceives as 'professional' is, up close, just this agreement kept.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is MockFrame really free with no watermark?

Yes — unlimited use, no signup, and exports carry no watermark. The PNG you download is exactly the canvas you composed.

Are my screenshots uploaded to a server?

Never. All rendering happens on a canvas in your browser using your device's own processing. Nothing is transmitted, stored or logged.

Which export size should I use for social media?

1200×630 suits link shares and blog headers, 1080×1080 is the classic square feed post, and 1080×1350 portrait currently earns the most feed space. Compose once, then export each size you need.

Can I make app-store screenshots with this?

Yes — use the phone frame with generous padding and a brand-matched gradient, then add your benefit headline in any design tool. Framed store screenshots consistently look more professional than raw captures.

What image formats can I upload?

Any image your browser displays — PNG, JPG, WebP and most others. Screenshots in PNG give the crispest result because they carry no compression artefacts.

Why does my mockup look better with more padding?

Padding is negative space, and the eye reads it as quality. Cramped compositions feel accidental; a device floating in calm space feels designed. Try 64–96px for social exports.
